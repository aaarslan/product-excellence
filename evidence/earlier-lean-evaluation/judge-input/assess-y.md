## Recommendation

Do not commit to a six-month build yet. Run a small, time-boxed concierge test to establish whether faster quoting produces paid, repeat demand. The evidence supports investigating a problem, not building this solution.

## What the evidence says

- **8 of 12 support conversations mentioned tedious quote entry.** That is 67% of this selected sample, not 67% of the market. Support conversations are not a representative sample, and reporting frustration is not buying intent.
- **Three spreadsheets substantiate 15–35 minutes per complex quote.** They do not establish typical quote frequency, how much time is removable, or whether simpler quotes dominate usage. The five owners withholding details leave uncertainty, not evidence for or against demand.
- **Two offers to try something free are weak adoption evidence.** Nobody has agreed to pay. Willingness to change workflows, trust outputs, and return repeatedly remains untested.
- **Spreadsheets and email are the incumbent.** An assistant must outperform their flexibility and familiarity after setup, review, correction, and support, not merely generate a quote quickly.

## Next move: a two-week decision test

Prepare a concierge pilot for 3–5 consenting shops with recurring complex quotes. Do not contact them until authorized. Keep most of the 30-shop audience available for a later independent check rather than exhausting it on an unvalidated offer.

1. Establish baseline quote frequency, active preparation time, errors, turnaround, and current workflow. Identify the repetitive step worth solving; a template or spreadsheet improvement may be enough.
2. Manually deliver draft quotes within the existing email/spreadsheet workflow. Use synthetic data for rehearsal and explicitly consented real data for operational testing. Require shop review before any customer-facing quote.
3. Measure net time saved including review and corrections, accuracy of prices and availability, repeat use, and operator/support minutes per quote. Separate manual pilot results from assumptions about future automation.
4. Present a concrete paid continuation offer. Record actual paid uptake when authorized, not hypothetical enthusiasm. Choose pricing before observing results, based on customer value and a credible cost model.

## Decision gate

Predeclare an illustrative hurdle: at least three shops use it repeatedly, save at least half their preparation time without critical errors, and accept paid continuation at a price supporting positive contribution after the roughly $1 generation cost, support, retries, and human review. Small numbers justify only a narrow next experiment, not market-wide conclusions.

If value is real but payment fails, test positioning or a simpler workflow improvement. If accuracy or support costs defeat the economics, stop. If the hurdle passes, build only the smallest proven step, then validate automation and retention before expanding scope.
